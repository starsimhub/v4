Products

🧠10% 🤖90%

Vaccination, screening, and treatment programs have two parts: what is given (a vaccine, a test, or a drug: a product), and the delivery, which says who gets it, when, and how many. Starsim keeps the two apart, because programs do: an intervention decides who gets something and when, while the product says what happens to a person when they get it. The same vaccine can then be given routinely to infants in one scenario and in a campaign in another, without defining the vaccine again.

This page covers products: vaccines, tests, and treatments, how their protection wanes, and products of your own. Deliveries (routine and campaign delivery, coverage, capacity, eligibility, and delivery triggered by events) and changes to a model’s parameters, such as distancing or school closures, which need no product, are on Interventions. The cost of products is on Costs. The tutorial T5 – Interventions and scenarios uses a vaccine for measles, and A4 – Interventions uses tests and treatment with agents.

A vaccination campaign

There is one delivery for each kind of product: ss.vaccinate(), ss.screen(), and ss.treat(). Each takes the product’s own keywords (a vaccine’s efficacy, a test’s sensitivity) and makes the product, along with how it is delivered. Here measles (an SEIR model with an R0 of about 15, latent for 10 days and infectious for 8) is brought into a town of 100,000 people, 90% of whom are immune, so that each case at the start infects about 1.5 others. A campaign reaches 80% of people with one dose of vaccine, which fully protects 93% of those who get it (leaky=False, all or nothing: see below), either on 1 May, three months before the outbreak would peak, or on 1 August, at its peak:

import numpy as np
import starsim as ss

S, E, I, R = ss.states('S E I R')
infection   = S >> E @ 1.9*I        # β = 1.9 per day
progression = E >> I @ ss.days(10)  # Latent for 10 days on average
recovery    = I >> R @ ss.days(8)   # Infectious for 8 days on average
measles = ss.Disease(infection, progression, recovery, init=dict(R=0.9), init_n=dict(I=10)) # 90% immune, 10 infectious

early = ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.93, leaky=False, coverage=0.8, at='2025-05-01')
late  = ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.93, leaky=False, coverage=0.8, at='2025-08-01')

kw = dict(total_pop=100_000, start='2025-01-01', stop='2026-01-01', verbose=0)
scenarios = {
    'No vaccination':       ss.Sim(measles, **kw),
    'Campaign on 1 May':    ss.Sim(measles, interventions=early, **kw),
    'Campaign on 1 August': ss.Sim(measles, interventions=late, **kw),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('measles.I')
msim.summarize().loc['measles_infection']
value
scenario
No vaccination 5855.651183
Campaign on 1 May 764.381106
Campaign on 1 August 3632.240361

A vaccine protects only those not yet infected, so the sooner the campaign, the more it prevents: on 1 May, it cuts the outbreak from about 6,000 infections to under 800; on 1 August, by the time it is given, most of the infections are under way, and it prevents about 2,200. Coverage (the share reached) and efficacy (the protection of those reached) are separate numbers, and both matter. Interventions are given to the sim with interventions=, singly or as a list. A delivery takes its name from the variable it is assigned to (early and late here), and has results of its own: the number delivered on each step, the number eligible, and the coverage reached (and cost, if the product has one). Name a delivery after the activity (vaccination, screening, treatment), since the product takes the name of the thing (vaccine, test, drug).

Products

Product Delivered by Main keywords

A product is made explicitly only where it is the topic, or where one product is given by several deliveries (see below). Every keyword of each is in the appendix.

The examples below this point use a curable infection with no lasting immunity, over 20 years, as an SIS model run as agents (since some of what follows depends on each person’s own history):

S, I = ss.states('S I')
infection = S >> I @ ss.peryear(2)*I  # β = 2 per year
recovery  = I >> S @ ss.years(1)      # Infected for a year on average, then susceptible again
sis = ss.Disease(infection, recovery, init_prev=0.1)

kw = dict(n_agents=5000, start=2000, stop=2020, dt=ss.months(1), verbose=0)

With R0 = 2 × 1 = 2, half of people are infected at any time once the infection is endemic.

Vaccines

A vaccine’s efficacy is its protection against infection: the chance of infection of everyone who holds it is multiplied by 1 − efficacy. A dict gives efficacy against several things: acquisition (the chance of infection), transmission (how infectious a vaccinated person is if infected), mortality (the chance of dying of the disease), or any named transition of the disease (whose rate it scales). A vaccine against several diseases is keyed by their names (efficacy=dict(hpv16=0.9975, hpv18=0.9975), with the delivery’s disease=['hpv16', 'hpv18']), and a vaccine that protects against the strains of a disease differently by an ss.by() over the strains (see Diseases). Below is every form of each.

By default a vaccine is leaky: everyone vaccinated is partly protected. With leaky=False it is all or nothing: a share efficacy of those vaccinated are fully protected, and the rest not at all. The two give the same protection on the first exposure, but differ after many exposures, since under a leaky vaccine everyone vaccinated is still infected eventually. waning= is how protection fades: a duration means it is lost after an exponentially distributed time with that mean. Here are all three, given to half of people in 2010:

kw_vx = dict(coverage=0.5, at=['2010'])
scenarios = {
    'No vaccine':                     ss.Sim(sis, **kw),
    'Leaky (80%)':                    ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, **kw_vx), **kw),
    'All or nothing (80%)':           ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, leaky=False, **kw_vx), **kw),
    'Leaky, waning after 3 years':    ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, waning=ss.years(3), **kw_vx), **kw),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('sis.I')

doses=[0.85, 1.0] gives a vaccine in doses, with the share of the full protection after each; each delivery to someone who already holds the vaccine gives the next dose.

Protection can also fade by its own shape. ss.box(dur=..., decay=...) is full protection for dur, then (with decay=) an exponential decline with that mean, or none; it runs under every solver. Any other shape is a function of the time since delivery, t, a duration (so t < ss.days(180) and t.years mean the same in any sim), returning the share of protection left; a function of (t, sim, uids) also gets the sim and the people who hold it, e.g. to read their age at vaccination (sim.people.age[uids] - t.years). A function runs as agents only. A dict gives each effect its own waning (waning=dict(acquisition=ss.box(dur=ss.years(1)), transmission=None)). A product given again to someone who holds it restarts a waning by shape or function.

scenarios = {
    'Box of 2 years, then a decline': ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, waning=ss.box(dur=ss.years(2), decay=ss.years(3)), **kw_vx), **kw),
    'Fading linearly over 5 years':   ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, waning=lambda t: np.clip(1 - t.years/5, 0, 1), **kw_vx), **kw),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('sis.I')

during= holds a product only while people are in some states: ss.Treatment(efficacy=dict(transmission=0.5), during=sis.I) makes people half as infectious while they are infected, and they stop holding it when they recover.

A product’s coverage is also a quantity a rate can read: itn.coverage is the share of the people in the same place who hold it, each counted by the protection they have left, and itn.coverage(fn) counts each by a function of the time since delivery (e.g. the share of mosquitoes a net kills). A disease made with the product can use it in any rate, e.g. Sv >> ss.null @ 1/life + a*kill*itn.coverage.

Tests and what follows them

A test (ss.Diagnostic) has a sensitivity, the chance that someone infected tests positive, and a specificity, the chance that someone not infected tests negative. ss.screen() delivers it, and its positive and negative are the people with each result on that step. What happens to them is another delivery, given them with who=, with its own coverage, so loss to follow-up is explicit. Here half of people are screened each year from 2010, and 80% of those who test positive are treated with a drug that cures 95%:

screening = ss.screen(sensitivity=0.9, specificity=0.98, coverage=0.5, interval=ss.years(1), start='2010')
treatment = ss.treat(cure=0.95, who=screening.positive, coverage=0.8)

sim = ss.Sim(sis, interventions=[screening, treatment], **kw)
sim.run()
sim.plot('sis')
print('Positive tests:', sim.results.screening.positive.sum())
print('Treated:', sim.results.treatment.delivered.sum())
Positive tests: 8806.0
Treated: 7060.0

A follow-up can have follow-ups of its own, to any depth. By default a test detects infection with the disease it is given for; detects= takes a condition for anything else. delay= on the test is the time from the test to its result.

A step runs after the steps whose events trigger it, so it acts on the step they happen; steps that trigger each other in a cycle can’t, and raise an error, unless one of them has delay= (as agents). A delayed step waits at least one step from its trigger (a wait drawn as less, including zero, ends on the next step), so it can be in a cycle, as in a test repeated a year after each negative result:

first  = ss.screen(sensitivity=0.9, specificity=0.98, at='2010', name='first')
retest = ss.screen(sensitivity=0.9, specificity=0.98, who=first.negative, delay=ss.years(1), name='retest')
retest.trigger = first.negative | retest.negative # Each negative result, of either test, starts a year's wait

A test’s values can be by the states of several diseases ({hpv16.I: 0.9, 'hpv18.CIN': 0.95}), including the boolean states of a disease written as code ('hpv16.cin'). With disease=[...], a value for a state without a disease ('cin') is for that state of each, and someone is tested once, in the most severe state they are in.

A test can have more than two results: results= gives them, most severe first, each with its chance for people in each state (None for people in none of them), and the last result is everyone the others don’t take. Each result is a result of the delivery and an event that can trigger what follows:

cytology = ss.Diagnostic(results=dict(high=dict(I=0.9), low={'I': 0.05, None: 0.02}, normal=None)) # Low grade for 2% of those not infected
screening = ss.screen(cytology, coverage=0.5, start='2010', interval=ss.years(1))
treatment = ss.treat(cure=0.8, who=screening.high)

sim = ss.Sim(sis, interventions=[screening, treatment], **kw)
sim.run()
print('High grade:', sim.results.screening.high.sum())
print('Low grade:', sim.results.screening.low.sum())
High grade: 8417.0
Low grade: 759.0

Treatments

A treatment’s cure is the chance that it clears the infection, once, when it is given: the person leaves the infected states by the disease’s recovery (transition= names it, when there are several). Its efficacy is what it does for as long as it is held, as for a vaccine: antiretroviral therapy for HIV is efficacy=dict(transmission=0.96, mortality=0.9). A treatment is given to the people who are infected, unless the delivery says otherwise. to= names the state cured people go to, when the disease recovers to more than one. cure=dict(cin=0.9) cures by state, of a declared disease or one written as code (which is cured by its clear_infection()).

Here are the two kinds of treatment, each given to infected people at a rate of 50% a year: a cure, and a treatment that cuts how infectious people are by 90% while they are infected (and are treated), without shortening their infection:

cure = ss.treat(cure=0.9, coverage=ss.peryear(0.5))
suppress = ss.treat(efficacy=dict(transmission=0.9), during=sis.I, coverage=ss.peryear(0.5))

scenarios = {
    'No treatment':                ss.Sim(sis, **kw),
    'Cure':                        ss.Sim(sis, interventions=cure, **kw),
    'Less infectious while held':  ss.Sim(sis, interventions=suppress, **kw),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('sis.I')

Both lower prevalence by about half. A cure does it by shortening infections, and reaches each person afresh each time they are infected; the other keeps people infected, but stops most of them from passing it on.

Treatment outcomes and resistance

A treatment that cures has three outcomes on each step, which are results of its delivery and events that can trigger what follows, as a test’s results are: cured, failed (infected, and not cured), and unneeded (given to someone not infected, e.g. after a false positive). ss.treat(..., who=treatment.failed) gives a second-line drug to those the first failed.

moves= names the transitions a treatment moves people along, instead of a cure by the disease’s recovery. These are usually transitions with no rate (cure = I >> S, with no @), which only treatment drives, each with its own probability: ss.Treatment(moves={syph.cure_early: 0.98, syph.cure_late: 0.95}) cures each stage of syphilis with its own chance, and ss.Treatment(moves={ng.cure: 0.95, ct.cure: 0.95}) is one kit that cures two diseases. A person in no source of the transitions is unneeded.

Resistance to a drug is then a transition of the disease, triggered by the drug’s failures. Here a gonorrhea-like infection has a strain that is resistant to the drug (Ir), which arises in 20% of the people the drug fails, and which the drug doesn’t cure. Half of the people with a sensitive infection are treated each year from 2005, with a drug that cures 95% of them:

S, I, Ir = ss.states('S I Ir')
infection     = S >> I  @ ss.peryear(2)*I   # Sensitive infections
infection_res = S >> Ir @ ss.peryear(2)*Ir  # Resistant infections, as transmissible
recovery      = I  >> S @ ss.years(1)
recovery_res  = Ir >> S @ ss.years(1)
cure          = I  >> S                     # No rate: only treatment moves people along it
drug = ss.Treatment(moves={cure: 0.95})
resistance = ss.Transition(I, Ir, where=drug.failed, p=0.2) # Resistance arises in 20% of failures
ng = ss.Disease(infection, infection_res, recovery, recovery_res, cure, resistance, init_n=dict(I=500), name='ng') # 500 sensitive infections at the start

treatment = ss.treat(drug, coverage=ss.peryear(0.5), start='2005') # Given to the people in the source of cure, I
sim = ss.Sim(ng, interventions=treatment, n_agents=5000, start=2000, stop=2030, dt=ss.months(1), verbose=0)
sim.run()
sim.plot(['ng.I', 'ng.Ir'])
res = sim.results.treatment
print('Cured:', round(res.cured.sum()), '- failed:', round(res.failed.sum()), '- resistant infections in 2030:', round(sim.results.ng.Ir[-1]))
Cured: 8091 - failed: 431 - resistant infections in 2030: 2609

Treatment first cuts prevalence, but each failure is a chance for resistance to arise, and the drug then clears the sensitive strain and leaves the resistant one: within about 20 years, the resistant strain has replaced it, and infection is back to where it was without treatment. A second-line drug (moves={cure_res: 0.9}, triggered by treatment.failed or given to those with Ir) is a second product. Resistance as one of several strains of a disease, with immunity between them, is on Diseases.

Costs

Every product takes cost=, the cost of each delivery, which is then a result of the delivery (cost), next to the number delivered. See Costs for costing a program and comparing costs and effects between scenarios.

One product, several deliveries

A product made on its own can be given by several deliveries, which then share it: a person vaccinated by either holds the same vaccine, and is not protected twice, and a delivery is not given to people who already hold its product (unless the product fades by a shape or a function, when a new dose restarts it). Here, in a population with births and deaths of 3% a year, a vaccine is given routinely to 80% of children as they turn one, from 2005, and in a campaign to half of everyone in 2010. The campaign is either a delivery of the same vaccine, or makes a vaccine of its own (ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, ...)), so that a child reached by both holds two:

kw_births = dict(kw, demographics=[ss.birth(rate=ss.peryear(0.03)), ss.death(rate=ss.peryear(0.03))])

vaccine  = ss.Vaccine(efficacy=0.8)
routine  = ss.vaccinate(vaccine, coverage=0.8, at_age=ss.years(1), start='2005')
campaign = ss.vaccinate(vaccine, coverage=0.5, at=['2010'])
separate = ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, coverage=0.5, at=['2010'], name='campaign') # Makes a second vaccine

scenarios = {
    'No vaccination':                     ss.Sim(sis, **kw_births),
    'Routine':                            ss.Sim(sis, interventions=routine, **kw_births),
    'Routine and campaign, one vaccine':  ss.Sim(sis, interventions=[routine, campaign], **kw_births),
    'Routine and campaign, two vaccines': ss.Sim(sis, interventions=[routine, separate], **kw_births),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('sis.I')
for sim in msim.sims[2:]:
    print(sim.label, '- doses in the campaign:', round(sim.results.campaign.delivered.sum()), '- routine doses:', round(sim.results.routine.delivered.sum()))
Routine and campaign, one vaccine - doses in the campaign: 2192 - routine doses: 1672
Routine and campaign, two vaccines - doses in the campaign: 2454 - routine doses: 1732

Routine delivery reaches each birth cohort in turn, so its effect grows over the years; the campaign protects everyone at once. With one vaccine, the campaign skips the children routine delivery has already reached, and routine delivery the children the campaign has reached, so it uses fewer doses than with two vaccines, where children reached by both are vaccinated twice and protected by both (1 − 0.2 × 0.2 = 96%), which lowers prevalence a little more. Which is right depends on the program: a second dose of the same vaccine is one product given twice (with doses=, above), and a different vaccine is a product of its own.

Each delivery has a name of its own, from its variable or from name=; steps made by a function, with no variable, are numbered (screen, screen_1, …).

Under compartments and agents

Products and deliveries work under every solver: as agents, the sim records who holds each product and since when, and in a compartmental run, holding a product becomes a stratification of the model. What follows each person’s own history needs agents: waning by a function of the time since delivery (an ss.box() runs under every solver, its box as several levels of holding the product), a test’s delay=, and a product written as code, as well as the deliveries listed on Interventions. A model that uses any of these runs as agents unless told otherwise. For any other waning shape under every solver (e.g. two rates in turn), write the protection as states of the disease, and move people into them with the delivery’s move= (e.g. ss.Delivery(move=S >> V, ...); see Interventions). A weighted count ((ss.by(biting=w)*I).n, A.n_trans) and a product’s coverage in a rate are worked out once a step in a compartmental run.

Writing a product as code

A product whose effect needs code of its own is a subclass of ss.Product (or of ss.Vaccine, ss.Diagnostic, or ss.Treatment) with an administer(uids) method, which is called with the people a delivery gave it to on each step. Like any module, it can have parameters of its own (with define_pars()), states, and results. Here is a vaccine that protects younger people more: 90% at birth, falling to none at 80:

class AgeVaccine(ss.Product):
    """ A vaccine whose protection falls with the age at which it is given """
    def __init__(self, efficacy=0.9, **kwargs):
        super().__init__()
        self.define_pars(efficacy=efficacy)
        self.update_pars(**kwargs)
        return

    def administer(self, uids):
        age = self.sim.people.age[uids]
        protection = self.pars.efficacy * np.clip(1 - age/80, 0, 1)
        self.sim.diseases.sis.rel_sus[uids] *= 1 - protection
        return

scenarios = {
    'No vaccine':                    ss.Sim(sis, **kw),
    'Vaccine, 90% at every age':     ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.9, coverage=0.5, at=['2010']), **kw),
    'Vaccine, falling with age':     ss.Sim(sis, interventions=ss.vaccinate(AgeVaccine(), coverage=0.5, at=['2010']), **kw),
}
msim = ss.parallel(scenarios)
msim.plot('sis.I')

A product written as code acts on agents, so a model with one runs as agents. Here administer() changes each person’s susceptibility directly, which lasts for the rest of the run; a protection that should wane, or combine with other products and modifiers without overwriting them, is better given as a vaccine’s efficacy= with waning=, or as an ss.modify() (see Interventions). administer() can also return outcomes, as a test does (dict(positive=uids)), which are then results and events of the delivery. An intervention whose logic is not a product at all is a subclass of ss.Intervention: see Interventions.

Pitfalls

  • One product, one protection. Two deliveries of the same vaccine object share it: someone vaccinated by both is protected once. Two deliveries that make their own vaccines (ss.vaccinate(efficacy=0.8, ...) twice) give two vaccines, whose protections multiply for someone who gets both.
  • Coverage is not efficacy. Coverage is the share of eligible people who receive a product; efficacy is what it does for those who do. Halving either halves the protection of a leaky vaccine, but they are different questions for a program.

Appendix: every product and its keywords

The keywords of each product, from its documentation (the full text of each is in the API reference, Products):

import inspect, re
import pandas as pd

def keywords(cls):
    """ Each keyword in the Args of a class's docstring (and its bases'), with its meaning up to the first semicolon outside brackets """
    rows = {}
    for base in inspect.getmro(cls):
        if base in [ss.Module, ss.Product, ss.Intervention, ss.Connector]:
            break
        args = (inspect.getdoc(base) or '').partition('Args:\n')[2].split('\n\n')[0]
        for key, desc in re.findall(r'^    (\w+) \([^)]*\): (.*(?:\n {8,}.*)*)', args, flags=re.M):
            desc = ' '.join(desc.split())
            depth = 0
            for i, char in enumerate(desc):
                depth += (char in '([') - (char in ')]')
                if char == ';' and depth == 0:
                    desc = desc[:i]
                    break
            if key != 'kwargs':
                rows.setdefault(key, desc)
    return pd.DataFrame(rows.items(), columns=['Keyword', 'Meaning']).set_index('Keyword')

Appendix: the outcomes of products and deliveries

Each of these is an event, which can trigger a delivery (who= or where=), and a result of the delivery (the number on each step). Each also remembers, for each person, whether it happened (.ever), how often (.count), and how long ago (.since, agents only). An outcome of a product (test.positive) is for any delivery of it, and one of a delivery (screening.positive) for that delivery only.

held (a condition, not an event) A product, e.g. where=vaccine.held, or every product of a kind, ss.Vaccine.held Those who hold it now

The transitions of a disease (measles.infection, or a branch, tr.to(state)) and the events of pregnancy (pregnancy.birth) are events in the same way; see Interventions.
