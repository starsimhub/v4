Demographics

🧠5% 🤖95%

Over a single outbreak of a few months, the population barely changes, and most models leave out births and deaths. Over years it does change, and that matters for transmission: births bring in people who have never been infected, deaths remove people (including those who are immune), and the population ages. For measles, the rate at which births replenish the susceptible population sets how soon it can spread again after an epidemic.

This page covers births and deaths declared for a population, which work however the model is run; rates from data by country, by age, sex, and year; life tables; migration; pregnancy; and aging. The tutorial A3 – Demographics uses them on a whole country, People and agents covers the population at the start, and Branching and rescaling covers modeling a large population with fewer agents.

Births and deaths

Births and deaths for the whole population go in demographics=. ss.birth(rate=r) adds people at a crude birth rate r (a typed rate is per person, e.g. ss.peryear(0.03); a bare number is per year, and above 1 per 1,000 people, as ss.Births reads it, so 30 is the same), into the first state of each disease (here S); ss.death(rate=r) removes people from every state at rate r, read the same way. Here is the measles model of the tutorials over 30 years, with 30 births and 10 deaths per 1,000 people a year:

import starsim as ss

S, I, R = ss.states('S I R')
infection = S >> I @ 1.9*I       # β = 1.9 per day
recovery  = I >> R @ ss.days(8)   # Infectious for 8 days on average
measles = ss.Disease(infection, recovery, init_prev=0.001)

demographics = [
    ss.birth(rate=ss.peryear(0.03)), # 30 births per 1,000 people per year
    ss.death(rate=ss.peryear(0.01)), # 10 deaths per 1,000 people per year
]
pars = dict(total_pop=100_000, start='2000', stop='2030', dt=ss.days(1), verbose=0)
sim = ss.Sim(measles, demographics=demographics, **pars)
msim = ss.parallel({'No births or deaths': ss.Sim(measles, **pars), 'Births and deaths': sim})
fig = msim.plot('measles.I', log=True, do_show=False) # A log scale shows the later epidemics, which are far smaller than the first
fig.axes[0].set_ylim(0.1, 200_000)
fig

log=True plots on a log scale; do_show=False returns the figure without showing it, so it can be changed (here, the range of the axis) before it is shown.

Without births, the first epidemic leaves almost everyone immune, and measles is gone for good. With them, in the ODE, births add susceptible children until there are enough for measles to spread again, and the epidemics recur, smaller each time, until they settle at an endemic level.

The births and deaths are transitions of the disease, so they are among its results (sim.results.measles.births, sim.results.measles.deaths) and in describe(). With several diseases, the same people are born into and die from each of them.

In the ODE, though, a fraction of an infectious person is enough for measles to come back, and the sim says so when it runs:

sim = ss.Sim(measles, demographics=demographics, **pars)
sim.run(verbose=1);

The number infectious falls below one person within a few months. In a real town of 100,000 people, measles would have died out by then. A stochastic model, in which people are whole numbers and chance matters, shows this:

sim = ss.Sim(measles, solver='binomial', demographics=demographics, total_pop=100_000, start='2000', stop='2005', dt=ss.days(1), rand_seed=1)
sim.run()
sim.results.measles.I.plot()
Sim: measles, 100,000 people, as a stochastic compartmental model (chain binomial), 2000-01-01 to 2005-01-01
  measles: R0 = 15.2, with 100 infectious at the start
  Done in 0.0043 s; measles: 100,151 infections; at most 75,827 infectious, on 2000-01-08

Measles dies out after the first epidemic, and only comes back if it is brought in from outside. This is why measles persists without reintroduction only in large populations (above a “critical community size” of a few hundred thousand people). See Solvers and backends for more on when an ODE and a stochastic model differ.

A death from a disease, rather than from every state, is a transition of the disease: ss.death(I, rate=ss.peryear(0.1)) (see Diseases). demographics=True adds births and deaths at default rates (20 births and 10 deaths per 1,000 people a year). ss.birth() also takes sex=, the chance that a child is female, and, instead of a rate, per=, a transition whose flow drives the births, with n= children each time: ss.birth(per=pregnancy.delivery, n=2) gives twins at each delivery, and the same form gives births from any transition, e.g. the calving of a herd modeled as a disease.

A birth or death rate can also change over time, with ss.timeseries(). By default each value holds until the next; 'linear' interpolates between them. Here the birth rate falls from 40 to 20 per 1,000 a year over 30 years, compared with a birth rate that stays at 40:

deaths = ss.death(rate=ss.peryear(0.01))
constant = ss.birth(rate=ss.peryear(0.04))
falling = ss.birth(rate=ss.timeseries({'2000-01-01': ss.peryear(0.04), '2030-01-01': ss.peryear(0.02)}, mode='linear'))

pars = dict(total_pop=100_000, start='2000', stop='2030', dt=ss.months(1), verbose=0)
msim = ss.parallel({
    'Constant birth rate': ss.Sim(measles, demographics=[constant, deaths], **pars),
    'Falling birth rate': ss.Sim(measles, demographics=[falling, deaths], **pars),
})
msim.plot(['alive', 'measles.I'])

The population grows more slowly with the falling birth rate, and with fewer susceptible children born each year, the endemic level of measles is lower. The times of a series can also be years ({2000: ..., 2030: ...}): a bare number from 1900 on is a calendar year, and a smaller one a time in the sim’s unit after the start. See Parameters for ss.timeseries() and other parameters that vary over time.

Rates from data

Birth and death rates usually come as data: crude birth rates by year, and death rates by year, sex, and age. The demographics modules ss.Births and ss.Deaths take these, and the rates of the UN World Population Prospects for a country by name, which come with Starsim. With ss.people() for the population at the start (see People), this is the population of Kenya from 2000:

kenya = dict(
    people = ss.people('Kenya', year=2000, n_agents=20_000),
    demographics = [ss.Births(birth_rate='Kenya'), ss.Deaths(death_rate='Kenya')],
    start = 2000,
    stop = 2030,
    dt = ss.years(1),
)
sim = ss.Sim(**kenya)
sim.run()
sim.results.alive.plot()
sim.people.plot_ages();
Sim: births, deaths, as 20,000 agents, each 1,530 people, years 2000 to 2030
  Note: unit='year' (from dt)
  Done in 0.063 s

The demographics modules run as agents. They age people, give each newborn a mother of reproductive age (people.parent, a woman aged from min_age to max_age of ss.Births, 15 to 50 by default), and can run on a step of their own (e.g. dt=ss.years(1) for the demographics of a model whose diseases step daily). rel_birth= and rel_death= multiply all the rates, e.g. ss.Deaths(death_rate='Kenya', rel_death=1.2) for death rates 20% higher than the national ones, as in a poorer district, or for a scenario.

The model’s population can be checked against the UN’s own estimates of the population of Kenya:

for year in [2010, 2020, 2030]:
    print(year, 'model:', round(sim.results.alive[year - 2000]), 'UN:', round(ss.people('Kenya', year=year).named.total))
2010 model: 40915919 UN: 41598566
2020 model: 52293624 UN: 52217334
2030 model: 63538032 UN: 63102246

The model follows the UN’s estimates to within 2%. The UN’s rates that come with Starsim are estimates for single years, given every ten years, so ss.Births and ss.Deaths interpolate between them. The UN’s totals include migration, which this model leaves out; Kenya’s net migration is small (under 1 per 1,000 people a year), but for a country where it is large, it needs adding (see Migration).

Data of your own

Rates of your own are given as a table. Crude birth rates have a column for the year and one for the rate (ss.Births(birth_rate=df), with columns Year and CBR); death rates have columns for the year, sex, age group, and rate (ss.Deaths(death_rate=df), with columns Time, Sex, AgeGrpStart, and mx, and Sex being Male or Female). Time and Year can be used for each other; for other names, give metadata=, e.g. metadata=dict(data_cols=dict(value='rate')). Each rate holds from its year until the next year given.

Crude birth and death rates are usually given per 1,000 people, and death rates by age per person. A rate above 1 is read as per 1,000, and a rate below 1 as per person; a table is read by its median, so a death rate above 1 at the oldest ages doesn’t change how the rest are read. A rate that could be either (from 0.1 to 1, or above 1,000) gives a warning saying which was assumed. rate_units=1000 (per 1,000) or rate_units=1 (per person) says which, and silences it:

births = ss.Births(birth_rate=35)                   # 35 per 1,000 people per year
births = ss.Births(birth_rate=0.035)                # The same, per person
deaths = ss.Deaths(death_rate=df, rate_units=1)     # Death rates by age, per person

Declared rates (ss.birth(), ss.death()) are always per person.

Life tables

A life table says how many of a cohort die at each age. ss.death(lifetable=...) takes one and runs under every solver: as agents, each person’s age at death is drawn from it, given their age; in a compartmental model stratified by age, it gives a death rate for each age group. ss.Deaths(lifetable=...) also takes a table for each sex, and for each year from which it holds:

A life table can be made from death rates by age. Here it is made from the UN’s death rates for women in Kenya in 2020: the share of a cohort still alive at each age is exp(−Σ mx × the width of each age group before it), and the deaths in each group (dx) are the difference between the shares at its two ends:

import numpy as np

rates = ss.data.rates('Kenya', 'deaths')
mx = rates[(rates.Time == 2020) & (rates.Sex == 'Female')] # Death rates by age group (0, 1-4, 5-9, ..., 100+)
ages = mx.AgeGrpStart.values
width = np.diff(ages, append=ages[-1] + 5)
alive = np.exp(-np.cumsum(np.append(0, mx.mx.values[:-1]*width[:-1]))) # The share of a cohort alive at the start of each age group
dx = alive - np.append(alive[1:], 0) # The share who die in each (everyone left, in the last)
table = ss.lifetable(ages=ages, deaths=dx)

pars = dict(n_agents=5000, start=2000, stop=2060, dt=ss.years(1), verbose=0)
msim = ss.parallel({
    'Constant death rate': ss.Sim(demographics=[ss.Births(birth_rate=20), ss.death(rate=ss.peryear(0.02))], **pars),
    'Life table': ss.Sim(demographics=[ss.Births(birth_rate=20), ss.death(lifetable=table)], **pars),
})
for sim in msim.sims:
    age = sim.people.age
    print(sim.label, '– mean age:', age.mean(), 'share over 90:', (age > 90).mean())
Constant death rate – mean age: 46.556656 share over 90: 0.16842327150084316
Life table – mean age: 32.98202 share over 90: 0.003642250101173614

With a constant death rate, everyone has the same chance of dying each year whatever their age, so many people live implausibly long: after 60 years, a sixth of the population is over 90. With the life table, deaths come mostly in infancy and old age, under 1% are over 90, and the population is younger. (A model with ss.Deaths(death_rate='Kenya') gets the same death rates by age, and by sex and year as well, directly; a life table is for data that comes in this form, such as the UN’s complete life tables (from starsim_data, with ssd.download('Kenya', which='life_table')), or a table of age at death of a herd.)

Migration

People arriving from outside are ss.immigration(rate=r), where r is a number of people per unit time (not per person); as agents, they arrive with ages drawn from the population’s. People leaving are ss.emigration(rate=r), a rate per person; as agents, they are recorded as having left (results.emigrated), not as having died. Both go in demographics=. In a disease, they can also be for one state: ss.immigration(I, rate=ss.peryear(2)), among the disease’s transitions, brings in two infectious people a year, as importations of infection from outside.

The UN’s estimates of net migration (arrivals less departures, per 1,000 people a year) come with Starsim, as ss.data.rates(place, 'migration'). Albania has lost much of its population to emigration: here it is from 2000, with the UN’s birth and death rates, and with and without emigration at the UN’s rates of net migration (which are negative throughout, so they are rates of leaving, per person):

migration = ss.data.rates('Albania', 'migration') # Net migration per 1,000 people a year, every ten years
rows = migration[(migration.Time >= 2000) & (migration.Time <= 2030)]
leaving = ss.emigration(rate=ss.timeseries({year: ss.peryear(-rate/1000) for year, rate in zip(rows.Time, rows.CNMR)}, mode='linear'))
print(rows.to_string(index=False))

def make_albania(migration):
    """ Albania's population from 2000, with or without emigration """
    demographics = [ss.Births(birth_rate='Albania'), ss.Deaths(death_rate='Albania')] + migration
    return ss.Sim(people=ss.people('Albania', year=2000, n_agents=10_000), demographics=demographics, start=2000, stop=2030, dt=ss.years(1), verbose=0)

msim = ss.parallel({'Births and deaths': make_albania([]), 'With emigration': make_albania([leaving])})
for year in [2010, 2020, 2030]:
    model = [round(sim.results.alive[year - 2000]/1e6, 2) for sim in msim.sims]
    print(year, '- millions without emigration:', model[0], 'with:', model[1], 'UN:', round(ss.people('Albania', year=year).named.total/1e6, 2))
 Time    CNMR
 2000 -19.118

2010 - millions without emigration: 3.47 with: 2.89 UN: 2.93

2030 - millions without emigration: 3.73 with: 2.55 UN: 2.67

Without migration, the model has Albania growing, to 3.7 million by 2030, 40% more than the UN’s 2.7 million; with emigration, it follows the UN’s estimates to within 7%. The rest of the difference comes from the rates being for single years every ten years, interpolated between them: Albania’s emigration fell faster in the 2010s than a straight line from 2010 to 2020 says, so the model loses too many people in that decade. Since the UN’s rates are net, they cannot say who leaves (as agents, emigrants are chosen at random, while in reality most are young adults), and for a country with net immigration (a positive rate), ss.immigration() takes a number of people a year, which is the rate × the population, worked out by hand. For a model in which migration matters, use data on who migrates, by age and sex, if there is any. For movement between places within a model, see Geography.

Pregnancy

ss.Pregnancy models pregnancy itself, for models where it matters: mother-to-child transmission, maternal health, or contraception. Women conceive at age-specific fertility rates; a pregnancy ends in a delivery, a miscarriage, a stillbirth, an abortion, or a maternal death; and each delivery adds a child, whose mother is known. It runs as agents, and takes the place of ss.Births: given both, the births of the two add up, with a warning. Fertility rates by age come from the UN for a place by name, as for births and deaths (fertility_rate='Nigeria', per 1,000 women in each 5-year age group, by year), or as a table of your own (with columns Time, AgeGrp or AgeGrpStart, and ASFR), here also per 1,000 women:

import pandas as pd

fertility = pd.read_csv('test_data/nigeria_asfr.csv') # Age-specific fertility rates by year and single year of age
pregnancy = ss.Pregnancy(fertility_rate=fertility, rate_units=1000) # Per 1,000 women per year; or fertility_rate='Nigeria'

nigeria = dict(
    people = ss.people('Nigeria', year=2000, n_agents=5000),
    demographics = [pregnancy, ss.Deaths(death_rate='Nigeria')],
    start = 2000,
    stop = 2020,
    dt = ss.months(1),
    verbose = 0,
)
sim = ss.Sim(**nigeria)
sim.run()
sim.results.pregnancy.plot()

Pregnancy is declared, with states NP (not pregnant) and P (pregnant), so its transitions are events that other modules can use, as for a disease: conception, delivery (which happens to the mother), and birth (which happens to the newborn). Each is also a result (sim.results.pregnancy.conception, .birth, .miscarriage, .stillbirth, .abortion). Contraception, for example, is a modifier of conception, and an intervention can reach pregnant women (where=pregnancy.P) or newborns (who=pregnancy.birth; see Interventions):

pregnancy = ss.Pregnancy(fertility_rate=ss.peryear(80)) # 80 conceptions per 1,000 women a year, at ages 15-49
contraception = ss.modify(pregnancy.conception, multiply=0.5, start='2005') # Halves conception from 2005

pars = dict(demographics=pregnancy, n_agents=5000, start='2000', stop='2010', dt=ss.months(1), verbose=0)
scenarios = {
    'No contraception': ss.Sim(**pars),
    'Contraception': ss.Sim(**pars, interventions=contraception),
}
for label, sim in scenarios.items():
    sim.run()
    print(label, 'births:', sim.results.pregnancy.birth.sum())
No contraception births: 1045.0
Contraception births: 818.0

Mother-to-child transmission uses the networks that pregnancy makes between mother and child (ss.MaternalNet, or ss.PrenatalNet and ss.BreastfeedingNet to tell infection before and after birth apart); see Contact networks.

Pregnancy has more options, listed in the appendix, each a parameter of ss.Pregnancy: the duration of pregnancy (by default 32 to 42 weeks, as observed), losses (loss_rate, a miscarriage before loss_threshold and a stillbirth after it), abortions (p_abortion), maternal deaths during pregnancy (maternal_rate) and at delivery (p_maternal_death), infant deaths (infant_rate), twins (embryos_per_pregnancy), the sex ratio at birth (sex_ratio), primary infertility (p_infertile), preterm birth (with a risk by the mother’s age, rr_ptb_age), and breastfeeding (p_breastfeed and dur_breastfeed). As agents, each woman’s parity, lactating (breastfeeding), and the time since her last live birth (pregnancy.since_birth) can be read in conditions, e.g. for lactational amenorrhea, which lowers the chance of conceiving while breastfeeding in the first months after a birth:

lam = ss.modify(pregnancy.conception, multiply=0.1, where=pregnancy.lactating & (pregnancy.since_birth < ss.months(6)))

pregnancy.attr(name, dist, on='conception') draws a value for each woman at each pregnancy (e.g. the gestational age of her first antenatal visit), for conditions such as pregnancy.P & (pregnancy.P.time >= visit), and pregnancy.add_callback(pregnancy.birth, fn) calls code of your own at each event.

Aging

People age if the model has births or deaths, and not otherwise; use_aging=True or use_aging=False in the sim says otherwise. As agents, each person’s age goes up with each step. In a compartmental model stratified by age, people move from each age group to the next: if people are spread evenly over the ages in a group, at a rate of one over its width (a group 5 years wide is left at a rate of 1/5 per year), and for a named population, at the rate its age pyramid gives, as agents would age.

The API reference for this page: ss.Births, ss.Deaths, ss.Pregnancy.
