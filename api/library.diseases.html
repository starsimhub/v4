library.diseases

library.diseases

Example disease models: cholera, COVID, dengue, Ebola, gonorrhea, HIV, influenza, malaria, measles, pertussis, polio, RSV, syphilis, TB, typhoid, and a non-communicable disease (NCD), and Starsim 3’s SIR, SEIR, and SIS written as code.

These are illustrative, lightly-parameterized models intended as starting points for building your own disease modules, not as validated models for any specific setting. Each one says in its docstring where its structure and its default parameters came from. Core disease base classes (ss.Disease, ss.Infection, ss.SIR, etc.) live in starsim.diseases.

ART

library.diseases.ART(year, coverage, pars=None, **kwargs)

Scale up antiretroviral therapy over time.

Each timestep, agents infected art_delay ago are offered ART, and are treated with a probability interpolated from coverage at the corresponding year. Requires the HIV module.

Note: CRN safe.

AgentSEIR

library.diseases.AgentSEIR(pars=None, dur_exp=_, **kwargs)

An SEIR model written as code: Starsim 3’s ss.SEIR (see ssl.AgentSIR), with its lognormal dur_exp and dur_inf and p_death=0.01; for an SEIR model, use ss.SEIR

This class extends AgentSIR with an exposed state: agents who are infected but not yet infectious. exposed and infectious are the literal E and I compartments, and infected is derived from them: an agent is infected from the moment it acquires the infection, whether or not it is yet transmitting. So infected and prevalence count E plus I, while transmission depends on infectious alone.

Correspondingly, ti_exposed is the time of acquisition and ti_infectious is the time of becoming infectious. ti_infected is deliberately not defined, since it is too easily confused with ti_infectious.

Note: CRN safe.

AgentSIR

library.diseases.AgentSIR(
    pars=None,
    beta=_,
    init_prev=_,
    dur_inf=_,
    p_death=_,
    **kwargs,
)

An SIR model written as code: Starsim 3’s ss.SIR

This class implements a basic SIR model with states for susceptible, infected/infectious, and recovered, as per-agent arrays updated in code (see ss.Infection). It also includes deaths, and basic results. It is an example of writing a disease as code, and a base class for diseases with per-agent behavior (e.g. ssl.Ebola); for an SIR model, use the declared ss.SIR, which runs with every solver. A Starsim 3 subclass of ss.SIR that overrides its methods can subclass this class instead, unchanged.

Its defaults are Starsim 3’s: a lognormal dur_inf (a mean of 6 years, std 1 year) and p_death=0.01, where the declared ss.SIR is the textbook SIR, with an exponential dur_inf and no deaths. Use it to keep a Starsim 3 model’s parameters; its timing is the agent engine’s (see above), so each case is infectious for half a step longer on average than in Starsim 3.

Note: CRN safe.

p_death float/ss.bernoulli the probability of death from infection _

An SIS model with waning immunity, written as code: Starsim 3’s ss.SIS (see ssl.AgentSIR), with its lognormal dur_inf

This class implements a basic SIS model with states for susceptible, infected/infectious, and back to susceptible, with a per-agent immunity level that is boosted by each infection and wanes exponentially, which sets each agent’s rel_sus. COVID

library.diseases.COVID(
    *args,
    pars=None,
    strains=None,
    stratify=None,
    _names=None,
    **kwargs,
)

COVID-19, as a SECIR model: a chain of increasingly severe states, each of which some people leave by recovering

People who are infected are exposed (E), then infected without symptoms (INS), and then either recover or develop symptoms (ISy); of those, some become severe (ISev, in hospital), then critical (ICr, in intensive care), and some of those die. People without symptoms and with symptoms are both infectious, at rel_ins and rel_sym of the contact rate.

Source: the simple SECIR example of MEmilio (Kühn MJ et al., Mathematical Biosciences 2021, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mbs.2021.108648; examples/ode_secir_simple.cpp), from which every default here is taken: the mean time in each state, the share who recover at each step, and the relative infectiousness of people without symptoms and with symptoms.

This version is not age-structured. Since the severity of COVID-19 rises steeply with age, a real model would be: covid.stratify(age=[0, 20, 60], split=[0.25, 0.5, 0.25], adjust=dict(severe=[0.2, 1, 4])), with a contact matrix given to the sim (see the user guide on stratification).

Note: CRN safe as agents.

Parameters

Name Type Description Default
beta ss.per the contact rate (per contact, with a network) required
rel_ins float the infectiousness of people without symptoms, relative to the contact rate required
rel_sym float the infectiousness of people with symptoms, relative to the contact rate required
dur_exp ss.dur the mean time from infection to being infectious required
dur_ins ss.dur the mean time without symptoms required
dur_sym ss.dur the mean time with symptoms required
dur_sev ss.dur the mean time with severe disease required
dur_crit ss.dur the mean time with critical disease required
p_sym float the share of people without symptoms who go on to have symptoms required
p_sev float the share of people with symptoms whose disease becomes severe required
p_crit float the share of severe cases that become critical required
p_death float the share of critical cases who die required
kwargs dict passed to ss.Disease, e.g. init or name {}

Examples

Cholera

library.diseases.Cholera(pars=None, **kwargs)

Cholera, with both direct and environmental (waterborne) transmission.

An SEIR-type model in which exposed agents become infected, a fraction of whom become symptomatic; symptomatic agents may die, and everyone else recovers. Asymptomatic agents are infectious but shed far less bacteria (asymp_trans). In addition to person-to-person transmission via the network, infectious agents shed into a single well-mixed environmental reservoir, which decays exponentially and drives indirect transmission with a dose-response governed by half_sat_rate.

Parameter values are drawn from the literature; see the source for citations.

Note: CRN safe.

Ebola

library.diseases.Ebola(pars=None, **kwargs)

Ebola, including severe disease and transmission from unburied bodies.

Extends ssl.AgentSEIR with severe and buried states. Exposed agents become infectious, a fraction progress to severe disease, and a fraction of those die; everyone else recovers. Severe agents are more infectious (sev_factor), and dead agents remain infectious until buried (unburied_factor), with safe burials happening immediately and unsafe burials after a delay. Bodies are only removed from the population (i.e. counted as deaths by the sim) once they are buried.

Note: CRN safe.

Gonorrhea

library.diseases.Gonorrhea(
    *args,
    pars=None,
    strains=None,
    stratify=None,
    _names=None,
    **kwargs,
)

Gonorrhea, as an SIS model on a sexual network

Gonorrhea leaves no lasting immunity: people clear the infection, by treatment or on their own, and are susceptible again at once. Most infections in women go without symptoms and so last much longer than those in men, which is why a fuller model gives the duration and the transmission probability by sex.

Source: the parameters are those of the gonorrhea module of STIsim (https://github.com/starsimhub/stisim, stisim/diseases/gonorrhea.py), whose own sources are cited there: a transmission probability per act from men to women of 0.06, and durations to clearance of a few months to about a year, from which dur_inf here is a single average.

Nobody is treated here, so the level gonorrhea settles at is above what a setting with treatment would show, and it depends strongly on the network: with few partners and few acts it dies out instead.

Note: CRN safe as agents.

HIV

library.diseases.HIV(pars=None, **kwargs)

Simple HIV model with CD4 count dynamics and ART.

Infected agents have a CD4 count that declines towards cd4_min while untreated and recovers towards cd4_max while on ART, at a rate set by cd4_rate. The per-timestep probability of death scales with how far the CD4 count has fallen, so agents with low CD4 counts die soonest. Agents on ART have their transmissibility reduced by art_efficacy. Vertical transmission is supported: set_congenital() infects the newborn.

Use with ART to scale up treatment over time, and CD4_analyzer to record CD4 counts. Since beta defaults to 0, it must be set for transmission to occur.

Note: CRN safe.

art_efficacy float proportional reduction in transmission on ART required

death_dist Dist death probability, by default CD4-modulated p_death required
p_death rate baseline death rate per unit time (not per infection) required

Influenza

library.diseases.Influenza(
    *args,
    pars=None,
    strains=None,
    stratify=None,
    _names=None,
    **kwargs,
)

Seasonal influenza, as a SEIRS model with a yearly cycle in transmission

Transmission follows a smooth yearly cycle (ss.seasonal), highest in January in the northern hemisphere, and immunity wanes within a year or so, as the virus drifts. Together these keep influenza returning each winter. For the southern hemisphere, give the cycle another peak: ssl.Influenza(beta=ss.seasonal(mean=ss.perday(0.26), amplitude=0.3, peak='July')).

Sources: the natural history is from Carrat F et al., “Time lines of infection and disease in human influenza: a review of volunteer challenge studies”, Am J Epidemiol 2008;167(7):775-785 (https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwm375): about 2 days from infection to shedding, and about 5 days of shedding. beta gives an R0 of 1.3 on average over the year, the median estimate for seasonal influenza in the review of Biggerstaff M et al., BMC Infect Dis 2014;14:480 (https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2334-14-480); R0() reports 1.8, since it reads the cycle at the start of the run, which is its January peak. The amplitude of the cycle and the duration of immunity are illustrative, and since everyone here becomes fully susceptible again, the epidemics infect more people each year than real seasons do.

Note: CRN safe as agents.

dur_inf ss.dur the mean time infectious required

kwargs dict passed to ss.Disease, e.g. init or name {}

Malaria

library.diseases.Malaria(
    *args,
    pars=None,
    strains=None,
    stratify=None,
    _names=None,
    **kwargs,
)

Malaria as the Ross-Macdonald model of humans and mosquitoes

People are susceptible, infectious, or recovered, and are born and die; mosquitoes are susceptible or infectious, and stay infectious until they die. Mosquitoes are a count, not people (Sv, Iv), and start at their density of m per person, all susceptible.

Source: the Ross-Macdonald model, in the form of Keeling MJ, Rohani P, Modeling Infectious Diseases in Humans and Animals, Princeton University Press, 2008, section 4.4. Every default here is from its Program 4.4: the biting rate a (its r of 0.5 per 1,000 humans), the transmission probabilities b and c (its transmission matrix), gamma, mu_h, mu_v, and m (its 10,091 mosquitoes per 1,000 people). The model is originally that of Ross R, The Prevention of Malaria, Murray, 1911, and Macdonald G, The Epidemiology and Control of Malaria, Oxford University Press, 1957.

R0() is 14.6 with these values, which is the square root of the classical Ross-Macdonald value, m·a²·b·c/(γ·μ_v) = 213, since each passage through a mosquito and back is two generations.

MalariaImmunity

library.diseases.MalariaImmunity(
    *args,
    pars=None,
    strains=None,
    stratify=None,
    _names=None,
    **kwargs,
)

Malaria with immunity, as the Garki model

The states keep the source’s names: x1 (nonimmune and negative), x2 (nonimmune, incubating), y1 (positive and infectious), y2 (positive, not infectious, recovering slowly), y3 (immune positive, recovering fast), x3 (immune and negative), and x4 (immune, incubating). People lose infectivity before they gain a fast recovery rate, and are detected less often once immune (q3). Everything about the mosquitoes is in one number, the vectorial capacity C: the potentially infective contacts each person makes per day through them. Inoculation saturates as C rises (h = g(1 − exp(−C·y1))), and superinfection slows recovery (R(h) = h/(exp(h/r) − 1)).

Source: the Garki model of Dietz K, Molineaux L, Thomas A, “A malaria model tested in the African savannah”, Bull World Health Organ 1974;50(3-4):347-357, in the form given by Molineaux L, Gramiccia G, The Garki Project, WHO, 1980, chapter 10. The defaults are its Table 31: g, alpha2, and r1 were fitted to the Garki data, and alpha1, delta, dur_inc, ratio (r2/r1), and q3 were assumed. C varied by season and village from below 0.01 to above 2 (its Fig. 77), so the default here is a constant chosen for illustration; give an ss.seasonal() for a seasonal one.

Malaria dies out below the critical vectorial capacity, (alpha1 + delta)/g, which is 0.022 per day with these values, as the source gives.

The structure is that of Keeling MJ, Rohani P, Modeling Infectious Diseases in Humans and Animals, Princeton University Press, 2008, chapter 2.

Syphilis, as a staged infection, wholly declared

People who are infected incubate (E), then have a primary chancre (P), then secondary syphilis (Se), and then early latent syphilis (El), from which some relapse to secondary syphilis and the rest pass to late latent syphilis (Ll). A share of those develop tertiary syphilis (T), which can kill; the rest are no longer infectious (R).

How infectious a person with latent syphilis is falls with the time since their latent stage began, a quantity each person carries: two modifiers of rel_trans, members of the disease. Since it acts on each person, this disease runs as agents.

Source: the natural history and its parameters are those of the syphilis module of STIsim (https://github.com/starsimhub/stisim, stisim/diseases/syphilis.py), whose own sources are cited there: the incubation period, primary and secondary stages from Garnett GP et al. (1997), the definition of early latent syphilis as up to two years after infection from WHO Europe, and the share developing tertiary syphilis. The half-life of latent infectiousness is STIsim’s.

Nobody is treated here, and the latent and tertiary stages last for years and count towards prevalence, so the level it settles at is well above what a setting with treatment and screening would show; add ss.screen() and ss.treat() to the sim for that. How far it spreads also depends on the network: the partnerships, how long they last, and the acts in them.

Tuberculosis, as the LSHTM model of the spectrum of TB disease

People who are infected either clear the infection, or develop disease that moves back and forth between non-infectious, asymptomatic (infectious, but at trans_asymp of a symptomatic person), and symptomatic. People who cleared or recovered can be infected again, at their own relative rate. Only symptomatic people die of TB; every death is replaced by a birth, so the population stays the same size, as in the source.

Source: the LSHTM natural history of Schwalb A et al., PLOS Glob Public Health 2025, 5(1):e0005050 (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0005050), in the form of the reference ODE of TBsim (tbsim/compartmental/lshtm_ode.R), from which every default here is taken: beta, trans_asymp, the reinfection multipliers, the rates between states, and the background death rate. Treatment is off in that reference, and is left out here; TBsim adds treatment, drug resistance, and preventive therapy.

With the reference’s beta of 9, TB declines slowly over centuries (R0() is 0.23, since most infections are cleared and few reach disease), reproducing that reference exactly; beta is what a model of a particular place fits to its data.

Rates are per year, so a step of a year or a month suits it.

Dengue: several serotypes, each passed on by mosquitoes, with partial cross-protection between them

Each serotype is a disease of its own, den1 to den4, with the host and vector structure of ssl.Malaria, plus the extrinsic incubation period in the mosquito, so this returns a list of diseases, and the results are sim.results.den1 and so on. Having had one serotype gives complete protection against that one and partial protection against the others (cross), a modifier of each serotype’s rel_sus.

Sources: the host-vector structure is the Ross-Macdonald model as given by Keeling MJ, Rohani P, Modeling Infectious Diseases in Humans and Animals, Princeton University Press, 2008, section 4.4 (see ssl.Malaria). The dengue values are indicative ones from the literature rather than one source’s fitted set: about 6 days of viremia in people and an extrinsic incubation period of about 10 days in Aedes aegypti at tropical temperatures, with a mosquito lifetime of about 10 days. That infection with one serotype gives lasting protection against it and temporary, partial protection against the others is the finding of Reich NG et al., “Interactions between serotypes of dengue highlight epidemiological impact of cross-immunity”, J R Soc Interface 2013;10(86):20130414 (https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2013.0414); here that cross-protection does not wane, which is a simplification, and its size (cross) is illustrative.
